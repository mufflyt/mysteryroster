The scenario that starts every one of these sections

Your team links NPPES to Medicare PECOS through organizational group reassignments and reports that 54% of nurse-midwives have no valid practice address on file. Three weeks later a different analyst reruns the same join and gets 4%. Nobody touched the underlying data. The number moved because of a plumbing decision nobody wrote down: whether a clinician’s address counts as “missing” when it can only be recovered by following her employer’s billing group through a 20-site reassignment chain.

That is the failure mode behind every section below. Directory audits rarely break because two sources disagree – sources always disagree a little. They break because of invisible pipeline choices: a relational join that silently multiplies one clinician into thirteen suites across two states, a sub-feed that goes missing for exactly one panel year and reads as a mass exodus, two competing definitions of “100%” that both sound authoritative in print, or a three-way tie vote that abstains when coarser evidence could have resolved it outright. Each mistake is cheap to make and expensive to defend to a reviewer, an IRB, or the HHS Office of Inspector General.

This vignette fixes eight of them, one function at a time, each with real numbers from a toy cohort you can trace end to end:

Attribute Normalization and Format-Validity Guards

The trap: two directories can report the exact same ZIP code, phone number, or credential and still look like a disagreement, because one of them dropped a leading zero, formatted a phone number with dashes instead of parentheses, or spelled out “Certified Nurse Midwife” while the other wrote “CNM.” Every downstream comparison in this package inherits that noise unless it’s cleaned first.

The fix: attribute_record_presence() already distinguishes whether a field is "populated" from whether it’s well-formed. normalize_directory_attribute() goes one step further and runs canonical normalizers across all seven standard directory data types – restoring stripped leading zeros on 3- and 4-digit Northeast and Puerto Rico ZIP codes ("7030" \(\to\) "07030", "901" \(\to\) "00901"), validating 10-digit NANP phone numbers, and verifying the ISO/CMS Luhn check digit on 10-digit NPIs:

# 5-digit ZIP normalization with leading-zero restoration and placeholder rejection
normalize_directory_attribute(
  c("80204-1234", "7030", "901", "00000", "ZIP IS UNKNOWN", NA),
  normalizer = "zip5"
)

Why it matters: every section after this one compares values across sources. If the comparison runs on un-normalized strings, “disagreement” partly just means “different formatting conventions” – and that inflates every inconsistency rate downstream, including the headline numbers reconciled at the very end of this vignette.

Auditing Longitudinal Source Coverage for Sub-Feed Discontinuities

The trap: longitudinal source families are often stitched together from sub-feeds with their own, independent refresh schedules – historic Medicare PECOS enrollment extracts alongside Doctors and Clinicians (DAC) Care Compare snapshots, for instance. If one sub-feed is simply absent for a single panel year, the combined coverage rate craters for that year alone, and a careless read says “35% of clinicians left the workforce in 2020” when in fact a vendor just skipped a refresh.

The fix: attribute_source_vintage_audit() decomposes composite families by sub-feed and flags year-over-year percentage-point jumps (abs(yoy_pp_change) >= jump_threshold_pct) together with exactly which sub-feed went missing:

cohort_pop <- expand.grid(
  npi = sprintf("NPI%02d", 1:20),
  year = 2018:2021,
  stringsAsFactors = FALSE
)

# PECOS is present in 2018, 2019, and 2021, but omitted in 2020
pecos_subfeed <- data.frame(
  npi = c(sprintf("NPI%02d", 1:11), sprintf("NPI%02d", 1:12), sprintf("NPI%02d", 1:13)),
  year = c(rep(2018L, 11), rep(2019L, 12), rep(2021L, 13))
)

# DAC is present in all four years (covering 5 clinicians per year)
dac_subfeed <- data.frame(
  npi = rep(sprintf("NPI%02d", 1:5), 4),
  year = rep(2018:2021, each = 5)
)

nppes_feed <- cohort_pop

vintage_res <- attribute_source_vintage_audit(
  population = cohort_pop,
  sources = list(pecos = pecos_subfeed, dac = dac_subfeed, nppes = nppes_feed),
  source_families = list(medicare_combined = c("pecos", "dac")),
  jump_threshold_pct = 15.0
)

Why it matters: without this decomposition, 2020 reads as a genuine collapse in workforce participation. With it, the drop is correctly attributed to one sub-feed’s refresh schedule – a one-sentence footnote instead of a false headline finding. This exact scenario resurfaces later as a line item in the audit reconciliation log, near the end of this vignette.

Enrollment Cardinality, Location Specificity, and Billing-Group Topology

The trap: in Medicare PECOS, most nurse-midwives and allied clinicians don’t carry a practice address on their own enrollment record at all – instead, they reassign benefits to an organization, and that organization carries the addresses. Following the reassignment link recovers locations for nearly the whole cohort, but it also means a clinician whose hospital system operates a Wyoming clinic she has never set foot in now shows up linked to Cheyenne, Wyoming. A single relational join can turn one clinician into thirteen suites spanning two states, and nothing about the raw row count tells you that happened.

Here is the toy cohort the rest of this section works from: six clinicians, one (NPI 1) with a direct practice address, four who reassign through billing groups of increasing size, and one (NPI 6) with no address linked at all. linked_pecos and nppes_ref_state are reused below for the join-cardinality audit, the location-specificity tiers, and the billing-group topology in turn, so they’re only built once:

  year = 2019L,
  enrollment_id = c("I1", "I2", "I3", "I4", "I5", "I6")
)

Mutually Exclusive Geographic Concordance & Continuous Spatial Distance

The trap: a clinician’s board license often records a mailing or residential address, not her practice address, so comparing it to a practice-address directory produces real disagreements for reasons that have nothing to do with data quality. Automated Accounting-Identity Validation & Audit Reconciliation Log

The trap: manuscript numbers change between drafts for good reasons all the time – a definition gets fixed, a data vintage gets corrected – but if that change isn’t logged anywhere, a reviewer (or your own future self) has no way to tell a legitimate correction from an unexplained drift in the numbers.

The fix: attribute_audit_diff() verifies that every mutually exclusive table in this vignette (geo_conc$mutually_exclusive, spec_audit$tiers, traj_out$summary, markov_out$regime_summary) still sums to 100.0%, and generates a structured, reasoned log comparing baseline and revised manuscript metrics side by side:

Notice board_concordance_sum_pct: the baseline draft’s 103.58% is exactly the kind of overlapping-marginal arithmetic error Figure 4, above, exists to prevent, and the revised draft’s clean 100.00% is what replacing it with the mutually exclusive hierarchy actually buys. medicare_2018_coverage_pct is the missing-sub-feed story from earlier in this vignette, seen from a different angle – the jump from 22.71% to 46.84% is not a data problem, it’s a definition fix, and the reconciliation log says so in plain words instead of leaving a reviewer to wonder.

Why it matters: every number in this vignette ultimately has to survive contact with a skeptical reader. The reconciliation log is what lets you say, with a straight face, exactly why a number changed between two drafts of the same manuscript – and prove, mechanically, that the tables behind it still add up.

What this vignette does and doesn’t establish

None of the functions above establish ground truth on their own. A clinician’s directory listing can pass every check in this vignette – correctly formatted, pinned to one ZIP by every corroborating source, with a clean reconciliation log – and still be wrong, if the underlying practice address is simply stale in every source at once. The hierarchical truth fusion above only resolves ties among the sources it’s actually given; for genuine N-way consensus questions – how much of a larger group agrees, whether that agreement holds up over time, which source the group tends to converge toward – see vignette("consensus-and-corroboration"). The geographic and spatial concordance checks here assume the basic pairwise agreement and freshness primitives from vignette("attribute-agreement") already ran cleanly; this vignette picks up at the point where a flat match/no-match rate stops being enough. And the mover/stayer and disagreement-trajectory machinery above is a longitudinal complement to, not a replacement for, the lead-lag and catch-up-curve approaches in vignette("lead-lag-staleness") – use that vignette to ask which source is ahead; use this one to ask whether a change in state represents a permanent move or a daily commute.
